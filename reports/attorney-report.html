Progress report · prepared August 30, 2026

The Right-to-Let Record

A systematic recovery of two centuries of American case law on letting one's home for compensation — built for the historical-evidence tests that decide short-term-rental litigation, with verification machinery designed to survive a methods cross-examination.

Status caveat. Nothing in this report is work-product ready. No case has passed a full KeyCite/Shepard's check (a machine pre-screen has narrowed that work; see §6). Quotations are machine-verified verbatim against source text; legal characterizations have received one round of human review. This is a research record in progress, not a memorandum of law.

1The problem: the winning tests demand evidence no search can reach

Constitutional challenges to STR ordinances are mostly losing on federal theories and winning, where they win, on historical ones — state retroactivity clauses (Zaatari v. City of Austin), state due-course-of-law review, and any future Glucksberg-style claim that the right is "deeply rooted in history and tradition." These tests are won or lost on the depth and specificity of the historical record a litigant can assemble.

The record exists — but it is written in vocabulary that vanished. Nobody before roughly 2010 said "short-term rental." We indexed the complete published case law of four states and measured it:

Each era names the same practice differently; the modern term appears only after 1970. A Westlaw query built from today's vocabulary returns silence from the very eras that prove the tradition — which is how a federal opinion can assert "no fundamental right to lease one's property for less than 30 days" without engaging two centuries of lodger law. Recovering the record is a language problem before it is a legal problem. That is what this system solves.

Complete corpus, page-faithful

Harvard's scans of the printed reporters, ingested with original page numbers preserved as data — so every quotation carries a real pin cite.

Why it holds up — completeness is documented, not asserted: a coverage ledger records that every search ran over every era-and-state slice of the corpus.

Two kinds of retrieval, measured against an answer key

Exact-phrase searches carry each era's own vocabulary; a semantic index finds cases by situation ("a householder lets furnished rooms for compensation") even when no known term appears. Performance is scored against an answer key of cases expert lawyers already found by hand — real STR briefs plus the classic landlord-tenant treatises.

Why it holds up — recall is a published number (72% of treatise-anchor cases found; rising each cycle), every miss requires a written postmortem and fix, and no change is accepted that lowers the score.

AI reading behind a verbatim quote gate

AI readers fill a fixed legal questionnaire for each candidate — who was letting, for how long, how the court classified it, which side it helps — and every answer must be supported by a quotation. A mechanical gate then requires each quotation to match the original opinion word-for-word; a failed quote voids every conclusion that rested on it.

Why it holds up — invented quotations cannot survive by construction. In practice: of 649 quotations extracted, 646 verified (three discarded, and every voided claim re-read from scratch).

Independent second reader, human adjudication

A second AI from a different maker re-reads a sample blind; disagreements — about 16% of judgment calls, mostly on which side a case helps — go to a human with a third reader's written recommendation. All 89 flagged items in this cycle received a human decision, recorded permanently.

Why it holds up — the disagreement rate is disclosed, not hidden, and the final call on every contested judgment is a person's.

3What the record shows so far

One reading cycle — 712 opinions read of 11,191 candidates (6%, the pilot budget) — produced an adjudicated ledger of 204 relevant cases spanning 1799 to 2019: 121 favorable, 48 adverse, 34 mixed. The arc is continuous:

  • 1799–1845

    Boarders and lodgers are ordinary legal categories: New York and Pennsylvania courts build property-protection and status rules around them (Riddle v. Welden, 5 Whart. 9 (Pa. 1839); Wilson v. Martin, 1 Denio 602 (N.Y. 1845)).

  • 1858

    Howth v. Franklin, 20 Tex. 798: a householder taking paying guests is legally distinct from an innkeeper — the era's own homestay/hotel line.

  • 1907

    A Louisiana owner lets his lakeside camp "to all comers, including for single days" — litigated without anyone doubting he could (119 La. 1025).

  • 1916–1926

    Courts in N.Y. and Texas hold that taking paying occupants does not breach "dwelling only" restrictions; a widow letting fifteen of her sixteen rooms still occupies "for dwelling purposes" (114 Misc. 106 (1921); 288 S.W. 180 (Tex. 1926)).

  • 1958

    The Southampton line draws the enduring boundary: incidental renting by a resident is fine; a rooming house as a business is not — the exact line today's STR fights re-litigate.

  • 2018–2019

    Tarr v. Timberwood Park (Tex.): "residential purposes" does not bar short-term leasing — now cited 59 times and holding. Slice of Life (Pa.): purely commercial, operator-absent rental excluded. The modern split restates the 150-year-old line.

The evidence table the framing battle needs

The dispositive question in these cases is how the right is described: the city says "operating an unlicensed hotel" (novel — government wins); the owner says "a householder letting rooms for short periods for pay" (ancient — owner wins, if provable). Proving the owner's description requires knowing, case by case, who was letting and for how long. That is now a table, with a verified quotation behind every cell:

The adverse file grew in parallel, deliberately: the same record shows a tradition of licensing and districting lodging houses. That is a manageable point (a tradition of regulation is itself evidence against a power of prohibition), but only if disclosed and briefed first — so the system hunts both sides.

4A quality episode, reported plainly

The first machine pass contained a serious doctrinal mistake: it marked several 2016–17 Pennsylvania Commonwealth Court decisions as favorable authority without registering that the Pennsylvania Supreme Court's Slice of Life decision (2019) had abrogated their reasoning. A human reviewer caught it. The correction is now recorded on every affected case, and a subsequent citation-graph check surfaced independent confirmation — a 2024 opinion stating outright that those decisions were "overruled."

We report this rather than bury it because it is the honest answer to the first question any lawyer should ask of machine-assisted research: what happens when it's wrong? Answer: the system is built on the assumption that first passes err — verbatim gates for fabrication, second readers for judgment, human review for doctrine, and a permanent record of every correction. The mistake cost one review session, not a brief.

5Why the method compounds

Each cycle feeds the next: period vocabulary the readers discover ("one housekeeping unit," the tourist-camp family) becomes next cycle's searches; the answer key grows; the master glossary of concepts, era terms, and anchor cases is a durable asset. Nothing is STR-specific — the same instrument serves any question of the form "what does the historical legal record actually say about X?": accessory dwelling units, home-based businesses, any tradition-and-history inquiry. And because jurisdiction is a first-class field, the output doubles as a state-by-state map of historical authority for venue selection.
